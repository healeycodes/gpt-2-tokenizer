At first light, the station clerk opened the west-facing window and wrote the
weather in a blue ledger: cool air, low clouds, three gulls above the ferry.
The entry was ordinary, but it gave the morning a useful shape. Commuters read
the board, folded maps into pockets, and carried paper cups down the platform.

By noon the clouds had separated into bright, uneven islands. A mechanic
repaired a loose door hinge while a child counted the painted bolts: one, two,
three, then thirty-two. The clerk answered the same question in several forms:
"The next train is delayed by twelve minutes." "Yes, the ticket remains
valid." "No, you do not need a new receipt."

Near sunset, a traveler left behind a small green notebook. Its first page
contained a shopping list, a telephone number with one crossed-out digit, and
the sentence, "Remember the quiet route home." Nothing else identified the
owner. The clerk placed it in the lost-property drawer beside a red umbrella,
two gloves that did not match, and a postcard from a town nobody on duty had
visited.

The late train arrived with its lamps reflected in the wet rails. Doors opened;
people stepped down, paused, and chose directions. For a few minutes, the
platform held footsteps, announcements, apologies, laughter, and the soft
mechanical click of a timetable turning to tomorrow.
